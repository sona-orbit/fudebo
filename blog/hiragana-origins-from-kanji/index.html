Hiragana Origins: The Kanji Behind All 46 Characters

Each hiragana character developed from a cursive form of a kanji. For example, あ came from 安, か from 加, and も still looks a lot like 毛. These origins can help you remember shapes, especially when two characters look alike. Here is the full chart for all 46 hiragana, plus ways to use it when characters are easy to mix up.

1. How hiragana came from cursive kanji

Early Japanese writing borrowed kanji to represent Japanese sounds. For example, 安 stood for a and 加 stood for ka. Using a kanji for its sound rather than its meaning is called man’yōgana.

As people wrote kanji quickly in cursive and simplified the shapes, they developed into the hiragana used today. Each hiragana has a source kanji. Some still look much like it; in others, the resemblance is hard to see.

Katakana developed differently. Its characters came from parts of kanji. For example, ア came from the left side, 阝, of 阿; イ came from 亻 in 伊; and カ came from 力 in 加. This article covers hiragana only.

A few notes on the characters in the chart:

  • The source for ね is 祢, written 禰 in traditional Chinese in Taiwan. Likewise, 礼 for れ is written 禮, 曽 for そ is written 曾, and 与 for よ is written 與. The source for ん, 无, is an old form of 無. The kana came from the forms shown first.
  • 川 is the usual source given for つ, though you may also see 州. 部 is the usual source given for へ, though some accounts say it came specifically from the right-hand part, 阝.

3. Hiragana that still look like their source kanji

Some hiragana still resemble their source kanji. Seeing the connection can make their shapes easier to remember:

KanaSourceWhat to look for
も毛It looks much like 毛.
せ世You can still see the structure of 世.
か加The left side comes from 力; the mark on the right comes from 口.
け計The left stroke comes from the 言 side; the right comes from 十.
に仁The left stroke comes from 亻, and 二 remains on the right.
う宇The top of 宇 has become a small mark, and 于 curves into the lower part.
あ安The top of 安 has shrunk into a horizontal and a vertical stroke; 女 has curled into a loop.
ふ不The strokes of 不 have spread apart.

You do not need to know Chinese to use these connections. If you can picture 加 when you see か, or 仁 when you see に, you have something to remember besides an unfamiliar shape.

4. Use origins to tell look-alike characters apart

Hiragana that look alike often come from different kanji. Put their origins side by side to help spot the differences:

Easy to mix upSourcesHow to remember them
ぬ／め奴／女奴 has 又 in addition to 女. Likewise, ぬ has an extra little loop that め does not.
る／ろ留／呂留 has 田 at the bottom, and る ends with an extra curl. ろ does not curl.
は／ほ波／保The left strokes come from 氵 and 亻. On the right, は has one horizontal stroke; ほ has two.
い／り以／利The strokes of い are about the same height. In り, the strokes come from 禾 and 刂, with a longer stroke on the right.
こ／に己／仁に has an extra stroke on the left. It comes from 亻 in 仁.
ね／れ／わ祢／礼／和Each has a different source. When you write them, watch the ending at the lower right: ね loops, れ sweeps outward, and わ curves inward.

Use origins to tell characters apart, not as a test to memorize. Even if you remember that ぬ came from 奴, you still need to see its reading and write it from memory to know whether you have learned it. For more on that, see the previous article: Can’t remember kana? Try writing them from memory.

5. When origins do not help

  • Some characters changed so much that the connection is hard to see, such as し (之), そ (曽), and ん (无). For these, focus on the kana shape itself.
  • An origin shows where a shape came from, not its stroke order. Learn the standard stroke order for each hiragana separately. It can differ from the kanji’s stroke order.
  • Origins are memory aids. You do not need to memorize the whole chart. Learn to write the kana first, then come back when you keep mixing some up.

7. Common questions

Do hiragana and katakana come from the same kanji?

Not always. Hiragana developed from cursive forms of whole kanji, while katakana came from parts of kanji. Some characters with the same sound share a source: か and カ both came from 加. Others do not: あ came from 安, while ア came from 阿.

Do I need to memorize the origins chart?

No. It is a tool for remembering shapes and telling similar characters apart. Learn to write the kana first, then check the chart when you keep mixing some up.

Why do some source kanji look different in traditional Chinese?

The kana came from the written forms used at the time. For example, the sources 祢 for ね, 礼 for れ, 曽 for そ, and 与 for よ are written 禰, 禮, 曾, and 與 in traditional Chinese in Taiwan.

Could learning the origins make me use the wrong stroke order?

Yes. Hiragana stroke order is based on the kana itself and does not always match the source kanji. Use origins to remember shapes, but practice the standard stroke order for each kana.
