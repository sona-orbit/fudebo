Hiragana Stroke Order: All 46 Kana and 12 Tricky Ones

Hiragana stroke order generally goes from top to bottom and left to right. Dots usually come last. Of the 46 kana, 11 take just 1 stroke. The most any take is 4 strokes (き, た, な, ふ, and ほ). Only about a dozen are especially easy to get wrong. For example, the curved stroke comes first in も, and the right vertical stroke comes before the left one in せ. Here's the full chart, followed by a closer look at each tricky kana.

1. 3 basic rules

Hiragana developed from cursive kanji, and its stroke order generally follows the original characters. Remember these 3 rules, and you'll get most kana right.

  • Go from top to bottom and left to right. For example, write the left stroke first in い and り. On the right side of こ and に, write the upper stroke first.
  • Dots usually come last. The upper-right dots in お, か, and む are the final strokes. The dots in う, え, ら, and ふ sit at the top, so write them first, following the top-to-bottom rule.
  • Write a crossed stroke before the vertical or diagonal stroke that crosses it. In あ, す, ま, and ほ, write the horizontal stroke before the stroke that passes down through it.

There aren't many exceptions, but も and せ are worth watching. Section 3 covers 12 kana whose stroke order is easy to mix up.

3. 12 kana with tricky stroke order

In each diagram, blue shows the stroke being written, gray shows completed strokes, and red dots show where to start each stroke and its order.

も: Write the curved stroke before the 2 horizontal strokes

It's easy to start with a horizontal stroke. Instead, write the long curved stroke first. Go down, then hook up to the right at the bottom. Add the 2 horizontal strokes from top to bottom.

せ: Right vertical stroke before left vertical stroke

Write the long horizontal stroke first. The 2nd stroke is the short vertical stroke on the right, ending with an upward hook to the left. The 3rd is the long stroke on the left, going down and then flattening out to the right.

お: Save the upper-right dot for last

Write the horizontal stroke first. For the 2nd stroke, go straight down, curve up to the left, then sweep around to the right in a large arc. Leave the loop open. Add the dot last.

か: The dot comes last here, too

The 1st stroke starts horizontally, turns down, and hooks up to the left at the end. For the 2nd, sweep diagonally from the top to the lower left, crossing the 1st stroke. The 3rd stroke is the dot on the right.

ふ: Top, middle, left, right

Its 4 strokes are the dot at the top, the middle stroke that curves down to the right and finishes to the left, the lower-left dot that flicks up to the right, and the dot on the right. Write the middle stroke before either side dot.

や: The dot is the 2nd stroke

The 1st stroke curves from the left toward the upper right, then turns down and back toward the lower left. The 2nd stroke is the short dot at the top. For the 3rd, draw a long diagonal stroke from the upper left down through the 1st stroke.

を: 3 strokes, with a final stroke like と

Start with the horizontal stroke. For the 2nd, sweep from the top toward the lower left, turn back toward the upper right, then turn down to finish. The 3rd stroke curves from the upper right toward the lower left, then finishes to the right.

ゆ: Start with the looping stroke on the left

For the 1st stroke, go down on the left, flick up at the end, then loop in a large arc to the right and back toward the middle. The 2nd stroke goes down through the middle and sweeps toward the lower left at the end.

な: The dot is the 3rd stroke

On the left, write the horizontal stroke first, then sweep down to the left. The 3rd stroke is the upper-right dot. For the 4th, start below the dot, go down, make a small loop to the left at the bottom, and finish to the right.

ほ and ま: Does the vertical stroke stick out at the top?

In both kana, write the horizontal strokes before the vertical stroke that passes through them and makes a small loop at the bottom. ほ has an extra vertical stroke on the left. On its right side, the vertical stroke starts at the 1st horizontal stroke and does not stick out above it. In ま, it starts above the 1st horizontal stroke.

え: Write the 2nd stroke in one go

The 1st stroke is the short dot at the top. For the 2nd, draw a short horizontal line, turn down to the left, curve back up to the right, then finish toward the lower right. Don't lift your pen at the turn in the middle, or you'll make it 3 strokes.

4. Kana written in 1 continuous stroke

There are 11 kana written in 1 stroke. Don't lift your pen as you go around their turns.

KanaHow to write it
くGo from the upper right to the lower left, make a sharp turn, then go toward the lower right. Make a corner, not a rounded curve.
へGo a short way up to the right, make a sharp turn, then go a longer way down to the right. The peak sits to the left.
そMake a small Z-shaped turn at the top, continue into a long horizontal line, turn back toward the lower left, then curve down and around to the lower right. There's also a 2-stroke form.
てGo across to the right, turn back toward the lower left, and draw a curve that bulges left.
ひGo a short way right, turn down to the left, curve into a large bowl shape rising on the right, then lightly sweep down to the right.
る／ろThey start the same way. Finish る with a small loop at the bottom. Finish ろ toward the left without a loop.
んMake a long diagonal sweep down to the left, rise into a small arch, curve down to the right, then flick the end up to the right.

The same idea applies to parts of some 2-stroke kana. In ね, れ, and わ, the 2nd stroke starts with a short horizontal line, turns down to the left across the vertical stroke, then loops up to the right. Only their endings differ.

5. 3 ways to finish a stroke

Japanese handwriting uses 3 kinds of stroke endings. Following the example helps each kana look right.

  • Tome (stop): Stop when you reach the end of the stroke. For example, the horizontal strokes in た and け.
  • Hane (hook): Flick up or to the side at the end. For example, the left stroke of い hooks lightly up to the right. The left vertical strokes in け, は, ほ, and に also hook up to the right.
  • Harai (sweep): Ease up as you move to the end of the stroke. For example, the end of ひ sweeps toward the lower right, and the tail of れ flicks out to the right.

A different ending won't make a kana unreadable, but it can make the shape look off. Don't hook the end of し too high. The difference between ね and れ is entirely in the ending.

6. Can you connect strokes?

Yes. Printed and handwritten kana differ, and a few kana are often written with 2 strokes connected as 1.

  • さ: Strokes 2 and 3 are often connected, making it 2 strokes.
  • き: Strokes 3 and 4 are often connected, making it 3 strokes.
  • ふ: Strokes 1 and 2 are often connected, making it 3 strokes.
  • そ: It can also go the other way. There's a form written in 2 strokes.

Both ways are correct. If you're just starting, I suggest using the standard stroke count first. It makes the structure clearer. You can connect strokes naturally once you're familiar with the kana.

7. How I practice stroke order with Fudebo

The hard part of practicing stroke order on paper is that no one tells you which stroke went wrong. I made Fudebo to let the app do that. It focuses only on handwriting.

  • Each new kana has a lesson card with a stroke order animation, writing tips, its origin, and kana that are easy to confuse with it. The writing tips in sections 3 and 4 come from those cards.
  • After the card, you trace the kana, write over a faint guide, then write it from memory in a blank square.
  • You write with your finger. The app checks each stroke's count, order, direction, and shape. If you make a mistake, the incorrect stroke turns red and the app plays the correct stroke order.
  • When the end of a stroke and the start of the next are naturally close, the app also accepts them as 1 connected stroke. So it won't mark the connected forms in section 6 as wrong.

8. Common questions

Do I have to follow standard hiragana stroke order?

I recommend it. Correct stroke order makes your writing neater and helps you read handwritten Japanese. But research suggests that the memory benefit mainly comes from the act of writing itself. If you don't get the stroke order right yet, keep going.

Which hiragana has the most strokes?

In the standard KanjiVG forms, the most is 4 strokes. The 5 kana are き, た, な, ふ, and ほ. There are 11 kana written in just 1 stroke.

Is さ written in 2 strokes or 3?

The standard form has 3 strokes. In handwriting, strokes 2 and 3 are often connected to make 2 strokes. Both are correct. The same applies to き (4 or 3 strokes) and ふ (4 or 3 strokes).

Why do you write the vertical stroke of も first?

も comes from the cursive form of the kanji 毛. In the standard stroke order, you write the long curved hook first, then the 2 horizontal strokes from top to bottom. KanjiVG lists them in that order, too.

Does katakana follow the same stroke order rules?

The basic rules are the same: top to bottom and left to right. Katakana has straighter lines. シ and ツ, and ソ and ン, are especially easy to mix up. The direction of the final stroke distinguishes them.
